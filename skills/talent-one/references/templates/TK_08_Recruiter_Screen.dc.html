Recruiter Screen Guide

Battery Systems Engineer · Mid level Harborlight Energy · non-technical recruiter, no engineer present

A script you can run cold, including three calibrated battery spot-checks with graded answer keys. You do not need to understand batteries: match what they say against the key and write down their exact phrases. That is a real, defensible signal.

01
Run of show

Thirty minutes, fully scripted

Bold text is said out loud; everything else is for you. The first two minutes set the agenda, and that is what earns you permission to interrupt later, so do not skip it to save time. There is no eligibility gate on this call: that time goes to the story and the spot-checks.

0–2
Open & agenda
"Thirty minutes. I'll cover the role and the team, ask you about battery work you've actually done, and leave time for your questions. Sound right?"
2–8
Story walkthrough
Career arc, current scope, and the hardware their battery work has actually shipped or been tested on.
8–17
Technical spot-checks
Three calibrated questions, graded against the keys in section 03. The highest-value nine minutes of the call.
17–22
Motivation
Why this company, what's missing today, and what would make them decline.
22–27
Logistics
Timeline, competing processes and their stages, comp expectations, location and relocation reality.
27–30
Sell & next steps
Deliver the sixty-second pitch, explain the loop, commit to a response date. Never "we'll be in touch."
02
Their work

Story and hardware walkthrough

Two prompts, then stay quiet. You are listening for whether they can name what they personally designed, analyzed, or tested, and whether they understand what breaks when battery hardware fails.

"Walk me through your path in about three minutes, with emphasis on the battery hardware your work has actually shipped or been tested on."
Listen for
Named programs, cell chemistries or pack types, test campaigns, and their specific piece of it. "I owned the cooling-plate redesign on our second abuse-test campaign" is what a good answer sounds like.
Red flag
Supervisory-only exposure. Cannot name what they personally designed or tested. Describes the team's work in first-person plural throughout.
"What's the highest-consequence battery component or test you've personally owned, and how did it fit into the larger system?"
Listen for
Consequence awareness (what fails, and how badly, if this is wrong) plus fluency about the system around their piece: feed, thermal, controls. Both matter more than the complexity itself.
Red flag
Describes scope as headcount or budget. No sense of what depended on the hardware, or what its failure mode was.
03
The core of it

Battery spot-checks, with answer keys

Three questions with graded keys. Read the question exactly as written, then match what you hear against the five bands. You are not judging whether the answer is right: you are locating it on a ladder an engineer calibrated.

Write down their actual phrases even when you don't follow them. Those phrases are what the battery interviewer uses to calibrate their own session, and they are the most valuable thing you produce on this call.

Spot-check 1 · thermal and electrical fundamentals
3 min
"What is energy density, and what does it tell you?"
What it actually tests  Whether they understand the core efficiency metric or just recite a definition. Energy density is the field's most basic figure of merit, so everyone claims to know it. A precise answer separates people who use the number from people who know what drives it. Role-derived from the O*NET electrical and thermal baseline.
No signal
No idea, or confuses it with power output.
Weak
"It's how good the battery is." Right neighborhood, no sense of what it measures or its units.
Good
Energy stored per unit of mass or volume, measured in watt-hours per kilogram or per litre. Higher means a lighter, smaller pack for the same job. This band advances.
Great
Separates cell-level from pack-level figures, explains why the pack number is always lower, and names typical ranges for the chemistries they have worked with.
Exceptional
Connects it to the trade against power, cycle life, and safety margin, and to where chasing density stops paying against cost or thermal risk. You are not expected to follow this. Write down their phrases, tick the box, tell the battery interviewer to raise their starting difficulty.
Spot-check 2 · the abuse-test tell
3 min
"What is thermal runaway, and why does it matter?"
What it actually tests  This is the single best proxy for genuine abuse-test experience in a short answer. Analysts and designers can define it; people who have actually tested packs have been scared by it. If they connect it to one cell's heat setting off its neighbours and a pack lost in seconds, they have done the real work. Role-derived from the cell-safety and test skills baseline.
No signal
No idea.
Weak
"The battery gets too hot." A guess in the right neighborhood, with no mechanism.
Good
A cell heats past the point where its own reactions keep generating heat, and it can spread to the cells around it if it is not contained. This band advances.
Great
Frames it as a self-feeding heat reaction, names cell spacing, thermal barriers, venting, or BMS cut-offs as countermeasures, and knows it can take a pack in seconds. This is the abuse-test tell. Often arrives with a war story: let them tell it.
Exceptional
Distinguishes onset temperatures across chemistries, or discusses propagation testing and nail-penetration results. Mark it and move on.
Spot-check 3 · the fast-charge transient
3 min
"Walk me through what happens in a pack from plug-in to a full fast charge."
What it actually tests  Whether they reason about the transient, not just the steady state. Datasheet performance lives at steady state; real packs are won or lost in the first minutes of a fast charge, when current limits, heat, and cell balance decide whether you get a full pack or a derated, overheating one. Author-generated, to reach the charging layer the fundamentals questions miss.
No signal
No idea, or "you plug it in and it fills up."
Weak
"Current flows in and the cells charge." A steady-state worldview. Not disqualifying alone, but note it.
Good
Describes a sequence: the charger and BMS agree on limits, current ramps up, the pack heats, and charging tapers as cells near full. This band advances.
Great
A real ordered sequence: pre-checks, a constant-current then constant-voltage phase, temperature-based derating, cell balancing near the top, and a clean handoff to idle. Bonus signal: raises unprompted what happens when one cell hits its limit early and how you stop safely. That instinct is the job.
Exceptional
Discusses lithium plating at low temperature, charge-rate modeling, or balancing-current limits. You are not expected to follow this. Note their phrases and raise the interviewer's starting difficulty.
How to score the three
Two at Good or better → advance. That is the bar; do not hold out for three strong answers.
One Great or Exceptional, the other two mixed → hold for hiring-manager review instead of forcing the call.
Two Bad answers → decline, regardless of the résumé. This is the one place you can say no on technical grounds.
04
Motivation

Motivation and honest fit

Four questions. Surface every deal-breaker now rather than at offer stage, and capture their own words for the hiring manager: paraphrasing loses the thing that makes it useful.

"What drew you to us, and to battery work here specifically?"
Listen for  Researched specifics: our hardware, the pack approach, a test they read about. "Clean energy is cool" is neutral, not negative. Specifics are a strong buy signal.
"What are you optimising for in your next role that you don't have today?"
Listen for  Alignment with what this role actually offers: full-subsystem ownership, test cadence, hardware proximity. Write their phrasing down verbatim and pass it to the hiring manager.
"What would make you decline an offer even if everything else looked right?"
Listen for  Deal-breakers now, not at offer: a comp floor, relocation limits, a title need, a timing constraint. Every one you learn here is a week you don't lose later.
"Most battery work here is on-site near the test hardware. How does that land for you?"
Listen for  Genuine feasibility, not politeness. Ask what a move would actually require and take the answer at face value. Do not infer from personal circumstances.
05
Logistics

Fields to capture, and how to ask

Ask these in one run so none of them feels loaded. Each has a private-notes field attached. If a field is empty at the end of the call, the screen isn't finished.

Field Say this
Location "Where are you based today, and if you're outside the metro, what would a move near the test hardware actually require for you?"
Timeline "If we ran a two-week loop, does anything in your world make that timing hard?"
Competing processes "Where else are you interviewing, and at what stage? I'm asking so we can pace correctly, not to pressure you."
Compensation "The published range for this role is $X to $YK base plus equity, level-dependent. How does that sit against your expectations?" 06
Close

The sixty-second pitch

Learn this, don't read it. The shape matters: facts first, then the constraint that makes the work interesting, then the honest cost, then a specific next step with a date.

"Quick picture of what you'd be walking into. A small battery team designing, building, and abuse-testing our own hardware, iterating fast between tests instead of waiting on a big-company release train. Small enough that one engineer owns a whole subsystem, not a slice.

And here's the part battery engineers care about: you follow your hardware from the whiteboard to the test rig and watch it run. When something breaks on the rig, it's yours to understand and fix, not a ticket handed to another group.

The honest cost is that this is hands-on and mostly on-site near the test hardware, and certification campaigns bring long days when a lab window is open.

Next step is a technical deep-dive with one of our battery engineers. I can have you scheduled within a week, and either way you'll hear from me by [Date]."

Caution

Before you score, check your own read. The affinity trap (over-crediting someone who reminds you of a past hire) and the halo or horn effect (letting one strong or weak moment color the whole call) both hit hardest on a cold screen. Grade each spot-check against its key on its own, not against your gut feeling about the person.

07
Decide

The call: advance, hold, or decline

Write it up the same day. Anything past four lines is you dodging the call, and the hiring manager reads only the first two anyway.

Before you hang up
·Spot-check grades logged, with their exact phrases quoted even where you couldn't follow them.
·One-sentence failure story in their words. This is the single line the hiring manager will actually read.
·Private notes complete: location and relocation, timeline, comp, competing processes, notice.
·Strong-signal phrases forwarded to the battery interviewer so they calibrate their starting difficulty.
·Candidate's own questions captured verbatim for the handoff; what they ask signals what they are really weighing.
·Recommendation logged: advance, hold, or decline, each with evidence you'd defend in a debrief. "Maybe" is not an option on this form.
Insight

Beyond the two-Bad-answers rule, you are not qualified to reject on battery depth from this call, and you should not try. Advance anyone who clears the spot-check bar and tells a real failure story; hold the genuinely ambiguous ones for the hiring manager. This talent pool is small, and a false negative here is invisible and expensive.
